\section{Incidents: wrong claims and what caught them}
\label{sec:incidents}

We recorded every case during the final phase in which a written or about-to-be-written statement
turned out to be wrong or misleading. Table~\ref{tab:incidents} lists the ten with what detected
each; Figure~\ref{fig:incidents} shows four of them as the agent saw them and as they were. The
detectors are of three kinds: \emph{artefact checks} (a validator or an experiment built to test
the statement), \emph{agent cross-checks} (an inconsistency between two things the agent had itself
produced), and \emph{human judgment} (the researcher looking at the output and objecting).

\paragraph{What the artefact checks caught}
Incidents 3, 5, 7 and 10 were caught by things that fail loudly: a Newton solve that does not
converge, a table full of $10^{-14}$, a purpose-built measurement, a validator. Incident~7
(Figure~\ref{fig:incidents}d) is the instructive one. The analysis section stated that the
fixed-tolerance runs satisfy the solver hypothesis with a modest constant; the agent wrote an
experiment whose only purpose was to measure that constant, and the measurement contradicted the
text by two orders of magnitude on the finest grid. The text was rewritten to report the
measurement. Where experiments are cheap and claimed constants are measured rather than argued
for, a class of overclaims becomes data.

\paragraph{What the agent's cross-checks caught}
Incidents 2, 6, 8 and 9 were caught because the agent had produced two things that had to agree
and did not: four error measures of which one behaved differently (Figure~\ref{fig:incidents}a),
a comparison whose magnitude called for a check at a shorter horizon, a fit that contradicted the
visible slope, and a sentence the methods section did not support. This is the habit that produced
the central theorem: redundancy the agent is required to reconcile.

\paragraph{What only the human caught}
Incidents 1 and 4, and the decision in Incident 3, needed a person. Incident 1 is a judgment of
quality that no validator expressed and the agent did not have: the manuscript satisfied every check
and was bad. Incident 3 (Figure~\ref{fig:incidents}b) was a modelling decision, keep the benchmark
as implemented and stop at $T=0.5$ or change the joint model, which the agent escalated with three
options and a recommendation to change the model; the human chose to keep it, and the limit was
documented. Incident 4 (Figure~\ref{fig:incidents}c) is the one we find most important. The agent
had seen the non-monotone velocity curve, written a plausible and wrong explanation (a tolerance
artefact of the harness), and moved on. The human's objection was not technical (``it looks
strange''). The investigation it triggered found that the harness starts Newton at the exact
solution and that the Jacobian is ill-conditioned by ten to thirteen orders, a fact about the
benchmark that no validator could have known to check. Looking at figures is not a formality.

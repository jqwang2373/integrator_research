\section{The research problem and its outcome}
\label{sec:problem}

\paragraph{Problem}
A mechanism of rigid bodies connected by lower-pair joints (revolute, prismatic, cylindrical,
spherical) is described in absolute coordinates by the position and rotation of every body. The
equations of motion are an index-3 differential-algebraic system on $(\mathbb R^3\times SO(3))^N$:
Newton--Euler balance for each body, algebraic constraints for each joint, Lagrange multipliers for
the joint reactions. An integrator has to keep the constraints satisfied at position level and at
their first and second time derivatives, keep rotations on the group, and handle joint friction,
which is regularized (Brown--McPhee) and can be sharp. Existing Lie-group methods for this class
are of order two, or are formulated for ordinary differential equations on the group; a recent
time-finite-element method reaches higher order on pendulum problems \citep{chaturvedi2026tfe}.
The program was started with the question whether a method of order higher than four, with a
provable order, exists for the full absolute-coordinate index-3 problem.

\paragraph{Outcome}
The method the program converged on, \method{}, solves one square nonlinear system per step in
which three-stage Gauss collocation of the joint coordinates, the joint constraints at position,
velocity and acceleration level, and the Newton--Euler balance at every stage appear as rows
($132$ for the two-body benchmark), and reconstructs the rotational endpoint through the
exponential map. The central result is that the reduced Gauss stage, i.e.\ the collocation solution
of the reduced joint-coordinate equations of motion lifted to absolute coordinates, satisfies every
one of the $132$ rows, and that every root of the non-dynamic rows on the regular branch is such a
lift. The method is therefore reduced Gauss collocation executed in absolute coordinates, its sixth
order follows from classical theory, and the two things the implementation adds (a velocity-level
endpoint closure and an inexact Newton solve) are $O(h^7)$ perturbations with explicit constants.
The identity, the perturbation chain and the Butcher conditions are proved in Lean~4 (57 theorems).
Numerically the method is sixth order on a frictional two-body chain and on the ASME double
pendulum, reproduces the three kinematically driven ASME mechanisms to roundoff, and reaches errors
below $10^{-10}$ where the public absolute-coordinate codes have errors of order one
\citep{wang2026gauss6}.

\paragraph{Why this is a good test of agentic research}
The result is not a benchmark score. It required choosing among method families, building
verifiers that did not exist, recognizing that a coding convention (joint-coordinate collocation
rows projected onto the current axis) was mathematically an identity, formalizing that identity,
designing experiments whose references and error measures are defensible, and writing for a
journal audience. Each of these is a place where an agent can produce something that looks right
and is not.

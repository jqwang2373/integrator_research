\begin{abstract}
We report a research program in computational mechanics that was carried out by a large-language-model
coding agent under human direction, from the first prototype to a manuscript with machine-checked
proofs, and we analyse what made the outcome trustworthy and where it nearly was not. Over four months
the agent produced 49 versioned experiments on Lie-group integrators for constrained multibody
systems, converged on a three-stage Gauss collocation step with full velocity- and acceleration-level
constraint enforcement, and then discovered, while auditing its own manuscript against its own code,
that the implemented stage system is exactly reduced Gauss collocation lifted to absolute coordinates.
That identity became the central theorem of the resulting paper; the agent formalized it and the
surrounding error analysis in Lean~4 (57 theorems, standard axioms only), designed and ran the
experiments, and wrote the manuscript. The process that made this possible is the subject of this
paper: a versioned exploration tree in which every hypothesis is a read-only directory, an evidence
ledger of 183 validators that pin every claim and number to an artefact, a proof sandbox, persistent
cross-session memory, explicit command and authorization boundaries, and a manuscript pipeline in
which every table entry is checked against the result file it comes from. We document ten incidents
in which a wrong claim was caught, and by what: four by validators or purpose-built experiments, four
by the agent's own cross-checks, and two only by the human reading the output. The human sent \nHuman{}
messages in the final four-day session against \nTools{} agent tool calls; the messages that mattered
were judgments of taste, scope and fairness rather than instructions. We release the repository,
the ledger and the session transcript.
\end{abstract}

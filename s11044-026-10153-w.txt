Higher-order integration of index-3 DAE with friction using
time ﬁnite elements on Lie groups

Ekansh Chaturvedi1 · Corina Sandu1 · Adrian Sandu2

Abstract
In multibody dynamics, Euler angles and Euler parameters (unit-quaternions) are common
choices of global orientation parameters. Both approaches have their limitations, e.g., gim-
bal lock and an extra normalization constraint, respectively. To circumvent these, the Lie-
group based frameworks methods employ SO(3) or SE(3) representation of rotations with
corresponding higher order Lie-group integration methods. However, quaternions represent
a much more compact representation of rotations and offer a computational advantage over
SO(3) or SE(3) representations. Implicit integration inevitably requires computation of Jaco-
bian matrices for Newton-Raphson iterations. However, deriving the component of Newton-
Raphson matrix corresponding to body-frame rotations within SO(3) formalism is not lim-
ited to calculation of partial derivatives. In fact, the process of deriving these matrices in-
volves a sequence of algebraic manipulations. Subsequently, they often need to be derived
for the acting forces and torques, on a case-by-case basis. It is this limitation that emerges as
a bottle-neck for modeling friction within Lie-group formalism, as friction loads are intricate
nonlinear functions of generalized coordinates (and hence quaternions), velocities as well as
Lagrange multipliers. To address this limitation, we propose a systematic partial-derivative
based approach for computing the sub-element coefficient matrices with respect to body-
frame Euler-rotations. This is achieved by defining general-purpose operators, as presented
in this work, that support convenient computer application due to accessibility to a wide
range of numerical and auto-differentiation tools. Further, recent advances in time-finite el-
ement based methods have demonstrated higher-order accurate solutions for nonlinear and
chaotic systems with relatively simple computer implementation. This work implements a
family of time-finite element integrators in conjunction with exponential integration of rota-
tions using Lie-group properties of quaternions. Further, numerical experiments showed that
the Lie-group time-finite element integrators compare favorably with Lie-group Newmark-β
and trapezoidal schemes.

1 Introduction

In the multibody formulations based on global-coordinates, spatial locations are defined
by Cartesian coordinates and global orientations are defined by unit quaternions or Euler-
angles. In such cases, carrying out higher-order fully-implicit integration simply requires
evaluation of Jacobian matrices, i.e., the partial derivatives with respect to generalized co-
ordinates. However, simulating multibody systems in absolute coordinates, where spatial
positions are defined by Cartesian coordinates and pose is defined by local rotations (also
called local integration coordinate), implicit integration requires evaluation of Jacobian ma-
trix (or equivalents) with respect to the body-frame Euler-rotation vector. Nevertheless, the
body-frame Euler-rotations are not generalized coordinates, and hence can not be used for
evaluating Jacobians. Instead, the recent work by Kissel et al. [1–3] rely on quantifying the
slight change in the vector of interest by evaluating the small change in the rotation matrix,
resulting in a matrix-valued operator Π. This provides a mapping between small changes in
rotation matrices, and those in orientations in body-fixed frame. This contribution is impor-
tant as the resulting first order sensitivities of kinematic constraints and constraint reaction
forces are vital in the context of implicit integration. In addition, the authors reported sig-
nificant improvement in computational performance with this approach, when compared to
the methods employing quaternions or Euler-angles. Moreover, these formulations are de-
scribed in standard matrix notation that aim to introduce the mainstream engineering com-
munity to powerful, yet abstract, Lie-group formulations. Although these contributions also
laid foundations for exploring higher-order integration, one important limitation that was
reported, was exclusion of friction from simulation. We observed that this limitation also
arises because evaluation of operator Π for complicated nonlinear functions requires ex-
plicit definition.
    For body-fixed constant vectors such as body-frame location vectors, evaluating these
coefficient matrices is relatively straightforward [1, 2]. However, for nonlinear and time-
varying vectors such as forces and torques, the process involves taking time-derivatives and
algebraic manipulations to evaluate the coefficient matrices on a case-by-case basis. When
the acting forces are represented by complex nonlinear functions of generalized coordinates,
for instance friction forces [4–6], analytical evaluation of respective coefficient matrices can
be tedious and often impossible for efficient computer applications. Due to this limitation,
the extending absolute-coordinate formulation, in its current form, to account for friction,
or any arbitrary functions of nonlinear forces dependent on generalized coordinates, can be
difficult.
    The absolute coordinate formulation [1–3] implements first-order Lie-integration on
SO(3) rotation matrices, and emphasizes on the scope of developing the formulation fur-
ther, for higher-order implicit integration. The existing literature on higher order implicit
Lie-group integrators includes Lie-group generalized α methods that are often limited to
second-order accuracy [7, 8]. Furthermore, Munthe-Kaas Runge-Kutta methods [9, 10] are
widely acclaimed for being A-stable even in the explicit form, and have been employed
for engineering applications [11]. Weiloch et al. [12] have further contributed second, third
and fourth order accurate BDF integrators on Lie groups. In addition, the authors, in [12],
claim that BDF methods feature reduced number of Lie-braket evaluations and hence are
more efficient than Munthe-Kaas methods. However, BDF integration methods have been

demonstrated on ℝ3 × SO(3) and SE(3) formalisms. The SE(3) group is the most natural and
unified representation of a rigid body pose and motion. It treats the entire 6-DOF motion as
an element of a single Lie group. In the context of constrained dynamics, the constraints can
be formulated directly on the full six dimensional state space or its velocity space, leverag-
ing powerful tools from differential geometry and Lie group theory [13]. The ℝ3 × SO(3)
representation, on the other hand, separates position and orientation, which is intuitive for
many applications. However, rotation matrices must maintain their orthogonality and unit-
determinant properties (9 elements with 6 constraints, leaving 3 DOF). While powerful for
standard kinematics, managing these implicit constraints during numerical integration or
when imposing additional motion constraints can be computationally intensive.
    The ℝ3 × S 3 representation of rigid body motion offers the most compact singularity-
free representation for rotation using quaternions, which is a major advantage over min-
imal parameterizations like Euler angles. It is often preferred in simulations and control
due to its numerical stability. The main trade-off is the need to explicitly enforce the unit-
norm constraint of the quaternion (4 elements with 1 constraint, leaving 3 DOF) during
calculations, which adds an algebraic constraint to the dynamics equations. Furthermore,
Terze et al. proposed redundancy-free Lie-integration in minimal form [11, 14], by circum-
venting the normalization constraint using Lie-group properties of unit quaternion group
S 3 = {p ∈ ℝ4 | pT p = 1}. In fact, for quaternions, the exponential map can be written in
an efficient closed form and numerically efficient truncations for inverse of differential of
exponential map can be performed as well [9]. For these listed advantages, it is worth utiliz-
ing powerful properties of quaternion Lie-groups to develop implicit integrators using this
approach. Recently, [4, 5] used first-order exponential integration on S 3 group for a unified
formalism for smooth and non-smooth dynamics problems, in optimization framework. Fur-
thermore, the availability of exact analytical expression for matrix exponential [4, 5, 11, 14]
assists in saving cost on Lie-bracket evaluations.

1.1 Review of time ﬁnite elements

Time finite element (TFE) methods based on Hamilton’s least action principle were first
introduced by Bailey [15] and Liepholtz [16]. In later years of that decade, Borri et al.
developed continuous TFE methods for multibody systems with large rotations, based on
SE (3) representation of positions and rotations [17–19]. These contributions introduced
evaluation of TFE discretized tangent matrices separately for each individual constraint and
assembling them later as diagonal entries. Subsequently, in addition to tedious computer
implementation, the resulting dimensionality of equations is larger than in joint-coordinate
formulation.
    In 1990s, discontinuous Galerkin methods were developed by Hulbert and Hughes, ap-
plying space-time finite element formulations to structural dynamics problems using classi-
cal polynomial interpolation schemes in both space and time [20, 21]. These methods were
based on Hamilton’s single-field and two-field principles and demonstrated high accuracy
with unconditionally stable time integration schemes exhibiting asymptotic annihilation.
    In most classical integration methods, the direct integration approaches such as
Newmark-β, generalized-α are preferred as they carry advantages of relatively easier com-
puter implementation, in contrast to Runge-Kutta methods that require state-space conver-
sions. A continuous TFE direct integration approach was developed for nonlinear structural-
dynamics problems by Lee and Kwak [22]. In 1996, Fung proposed higher-order L-stable
direct integration TFE methods based on Hermite polynomials [23].
    Methods that can be proven unconditionally stable for linear problems may lead to in-
stability when applied to nonlinear dynamical problems. Therefore, designing numerical
                                                                               E. Chaturvedi et al.

strategies for nonlinear dynamics is particularly challenging. The TFE approach as a gen-
eral class of integrators for nonlinear and chaotic problems was established in 2016 by Jog
et al. [24]. The study further revealed that a majority of existing methods recognized for
their robustness in addressing periodic, parabolic or hyperbolic problems can be formulated
within the framework of time finite elements. This approach, therefore, serves as a unifying
foundation for time-stepping techniques employed across various fields.
    In more recent years, the potential of TFE based integrators has again rejuvenated the
attention of researchers from various fields. Antonietti et al. [25, 26] have developed discon-
tinuous Galerkin spectral element methods for simulating seismic-wave propagation and
showed that the resulting discrete formulations are well-posed, stable, and retain a super-
optimal rate of convergence with respect to the step-size and polynomial order. Tamma et
al. [27] have presented Petrov-Galerkin based unified framework to design high-order al-
gorithms. This approach yields various unconditionally stable (A/L-stable) algorithms with
controllable numerical dissipation. However, the applications have been limited to linear
elastodynamics problems.
    Furthermore, Bauchau [28] has recently employed Hamilton’s two-field principle to de-
velop time-continuous and time-discontinuous TFE schemes for constrained multibody sys-
tems. However, the computer-implementation has been demonstrated in problem-specific
minimal coordinates, instead of more generic matrix formulations. In context of direct inte-
gration methods, Kim and Reddy [29] have proposed a TFE based direct integration method
that not only gives higher order accuracy for linear as well as nonlinear problems, but also
gives active numerical damping control using a single parameter. This scheme is based on
modified-weighted residual method where a single weighting function can be used to gener-
ate linearly-independent relations at all the nodes in the finite element system. However, the
method has been employed on structural-dynamics ordinary differential equations (ODEs).
When dealing with DAEs, order reduction with ODE integrators is a common phenomena
[30]. Hence, it is important to analyze the efficacy of TFE integrators in context of DAEs.

1.2 Contributions of this study

The focus of this work is the implementation of higher order integration schemes within
S 3 Lie-group DAE framework, while accounting friction in joints. Hence, the discussion on
Lie-theory is bypassed. The literature on Lie-group theory in context of dynamics is rich and
readers may refer to primary contributions such as [10, 11, 13, 14, 31–33] for more details.
To add context to the presented report, the efficacy of the approach [1–3] with its reported
computational advantages was acknowledged. However, two primary limitations of this ap-
proach were considered: (1) Evaluation of Jacobian-equivalent matrices for friction/arbitrary
nonlinear loads will require case-by-case transformations, as provided for kinematic con-
straints in [1–3]; (2) First order integration with the use of computed Jacobian-equivalent
matrices. Aiming at these two primary deliverables, the key contributions of the reported
work are as follows:
1. Since simulating friction is essential to multibody systems, and intricacies that arise with
   nonlinear loads need to be addressed to complete the Lie-group framework. The first con-
   tribution of the submitted manuscript provides a generic derivative-based definition of
   Jacobian-equivalent matrices for arbitrary nonlinear loads, including friction. This con-
   tribution to include friction in Lie-group based frameworks is novel and, to the best of
   our knowledge, has not been addressed in existing Lie-group frameworks.
2. Higher order direct numerical integration methods for index-3 DAEs are implemented
   in Lie-Group framework. For second order accuracy, implementation of Newmark-β,
   method is presented.
Higher-order implicit integration using time ﬁnite elements on Lie groups

3. A time-finite element (TFE) family of integrators for DAEs is formulated, targeting third
   and fifth order accuracy. The performance of the discussed numerical methods is demon-
   strated and compared using numerical experiments, focusing on the friction-induced ef-
   fects.
4. The performance of time-finite element integrators has not been studied considering
   friction-induced nonlinearity to the best of our knowledge. The presented analysis es-
   sentially aims to take a holistic approach of assessing these schemes in a Lie-group
   framework equipped with friction modeling capability.
    To the best of our knowledge, the above contributions are novel an have not been reported
in existing literature.
    The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 outlines the absolute-
coordinate index-3 DAE formulation with quaternions as generalized rotation coordinates
and utilizes their Lie-group properties to circumvent the additional normalization constraint.
Section 3 introduces a TFE based direct integration applied with S 3 group on index-3 DAEs.
A general methodology for nonlinear DAEs with friction is proposed and the formulation
of corresponding Newton-iteration is described in detail. In addition, the Sect. 3.3 demon-
strates the implementation of time-tested Newmark-β and trapezoidal integration schemes
in S 3 group formulation. Section 4 presents the numerical experiments on a rigid-pendulum
case study. The performance of numerical integrators is assessed on two cases: frictionless
pendulum and with friction in account. Section 5 concludes with key observations and a
discussion on further scope of the work.


2 Lie group formulation of DAE of multibody systems with friction

The index-3 DAE describing a constrained multibody system dynamics in absolute coordi-
nates (r–A formulation) is as follows:

                                                  M r̈ + 𝚽rT 𝝀 = f(r, A),                            (1a)
                                 J 𝝎˙ ′ + 𝝎˜ ′ J 𝝎′ + Π(𝚽)T 𝝀 = n′ (r, A),                           (1b)
                                                      𝚽(r, A) = 0.                                   (1c)

    Here, M ∈ ℝ3N×3N and J ∈ ℝ3N×3N are the global mass and inertia matrices, respec-
tively, of the complete system comprising of N rigid bodies. For each body i, the Carte-
sian coordinates ri ∈ ℝ3 denote the spatial position of the respective body, and the rota-
tion matrix Ai ∈ ℝ3×3 represents its orientation with respect to global reference frame. The
acting loads f and torques n′ are computed as functions of combined vector of spatial po-
sitions r = [r1T , r2T , . . . , rNT ]T , and orientations A = {A1 , A2 , . . . AN | Ai ∈ SO(3)}. With al-
gebraic constraints defined by the set of equations 𝚽(r, A) = 0 ∈ ℝC , the Jacobian matrix
𝚽r ∈ ℝC×3N represents partial derivatives with respect to Cartesian coordinates.
    The rotation matrix of ith body Ai is computed as a matrix-valued function of ei-
ther Euler-parameters pi ∈ S 3 , pi = [e0 , eT ]iT in r–p formulation, or of Euler-angles 𝝐 i ∈
ℝ3 , 𝝐 i = [ϕ, θ, ψ]T in r–𝝐 formulation [2, 3, 34]. The state variables in (1a)–(1c) comprise
of linear and angular accelerations r̈ ∈ ℝ3N and 𝝎˙ ′ ∈ ℝ3N , respectively, and the Lagrange
multipliers 𝝀 ∈ ℝC associated with the constraint manifold 𝚽 ∈ ℝC . Furthermore, the enti-
ties f and n′ represent the externally acting forces and torques on the bodies, respectively,
that are often nonlinear functions of the position and orientation variables. The operator

globally as s = As′ ), the operator Π essentially reflects how the perturbation in A changes
the orientation of s. As defined by Kissel et al. in [1–3], this infinitesimal change is computed

the coefficient matrix Π(𝚽) ∈ ℝC×3N maps the infinitesimal first-order perturbation in ori-

corrections in local orientation 𝜽 , 𝜽˜ ∈ 𝔰𝔬(3), are then used to update the rotation matrix A
at every iteration step, as delineated by Kissel et al. in [1–3]. Here, 𝔰𝔬(3) is the Lie-Algebra
of the SO(3) manifold parametrized by local rotations 𝜽 .

functions are included. In contrast to r–A formulation, r–p and r–𝝐 formulations are devoid
of this limitation as the equivalent coefficient matrices are simply computed as Jacobians
with respect to p and 𝝐 respectively. Hence, a general representation of these coefficient ma-
trices in absolute coordinates is an open problem. Because of wide availability of numerical
and automatic differentiation toolboxes, it is preferable to have the general representation as
a composition of derivatives with respect to the global orientation parameters p or 𝝐.
